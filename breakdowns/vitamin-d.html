Should you take vitamin D this winter?

Does vitamin D protect against cancer and heart disease? Let’s look at what the VITAL trial found, and where its limits matter.

The claim

Vitamin D can sometimes be talked about like a cure-all. This breakdown looks at the claim that taking it protects against cancer and heart disease.

The study

The VITAL trial was a randomised, placebo-controlled trial in the US. It included 25,871 participants: men aged 50 and over and women aged 55 and over. Participants took 2,000 IU of vitamin D3 a day or a placebo, and were followed for a median of 5.3 years. The trial also tested omega-3, but this breakdown covers vitamin D only.

What they found

For invasive cancer, there were 793 people in the vitamin D group and 824 in the placebo group. The trial found no significant difference. For major cardiovascular events — heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death — the counts were 396 and 409, respectively, with no significant difference.

A secondary analysis suggested fewer cancer deaths after the first 2 years were excluded. But it was one of many outcomes tested and wasn't adjusted for multiple comparisons, so treat it as a hint, not proof. The later D-Health trial in 2022 didn't find this. Later VITAL results found no reduction in fractures.

The catch

Participants weren't selected because they had low vitamin D. Their average starting level was about 31 ng/mL (around 77 nmol/L), and fewer than 13% were below 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L).

About 43% were already taking vitamin D supplements of up to 800 IU a day and could continue, so the placebo group got some vitamin D too. Blood levels were rechecked during the trial in only about 6,000 participants.

The trial tested one dose, and its participants were older US adults. The results may not apply to everyone.

Well, actually…

For people who already have enough, vitamin D isn't a magic shield against cancer or heart disease. Its main job is helping control calcium and phosphate, which keep bones, teeth and muscles healthy.

In the UK, between October and early March the sun isn't strong enough for our skin to make enough vitamin D. NHS advice is that everyone should consider taking a daily supplement of 10 micrograms (400 IU) in autumn and winter.

Some people should consider taking it all year round: people who aren't often outdoors, people in care homes, people who usually cover most of their skin outdoors, and people with dark skin, for example African, African-Caribbean or south Asian backgrounds.

More isn't better. Adults shouldn't take more than 100 micrograms (4,000 IU) a day, as too much can be harmful. 10 micrograms is enough for most people.

If you're unsure what's right for you, speak to your GP or pharmacist.
